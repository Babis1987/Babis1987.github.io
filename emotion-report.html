Summary

A real-time emotion-recognition system detects faces in an image or camera frame, classifies each face into seven basic emotions with confidence scores, and passes the result to a language model that writes a short natural-language description of the scene. The whole pipeline is served as a Flask web application deployed on Hugging Face Spaces.

Main findings

  • Speed over the last point of accuracy. MediaPipe (through DeepFace) and the compact mini-Xception classifier run on a CPU with a low memory footprint and stable real-time performance, at a small cost in accuracy against heavier detectors such as RetinaFace.
  • Clear expressions are recognised confidently (99.9 for a smiling face), while ambiguous ones spread the score across several emotions (36.11 sad, 31.42 happy, 25.33 angry for one face).
  • The system struggles with faces at the edge of the frame, low light and partial occlusion, many faces at different distances, and weakly expressed emotions; it also favours the frequent classes (happiness, sadness, anger).
  • Prompt design changes the description. One- and few-shot prompts score higher BERTScore against a reference sentence; BERTScore and perplexity do not always agree, because one measures closeness to a reference and the other the model's own confidence.

1. Model selection and pipeline

Emotion recognition runs in four conceptual steps (face detection, face normalisation, feature extraction, emotion classification). Because the system must work in real time, detection time and computational cost weighed into every choice:

  • Face detection: MediaPipe through the DeepFace library: slightly less accurate than state-of-the-art models such as RetinaFace, but much lighter and faster.
  • Normalisation and input preparation: the DeepFace preprocessing pipeline.
  • Features and classification: DeepFace's mini-Xception, which uses separable 2-D convolutions to cut the number of parameters. It is pre-trained on FER2013 and classifies seven basic emotions.

Only the bounding-box area is kept; DeepFace normalises the face.

05

Emotion recognition

mini-Xception returns a confidence score for each of the seven basic emotions.

OpenCV is the “engineering” layer: it reads images, extracts frames from a video stream and handles colour conversion and resizing. The pipeline returns a dictionary with everything needed to annotate the image: each face's bounding box, the dominant emotion with its confidence, and the scores of the other six emotions.

In the emotion-based music player, the emotion labels are mapped to tracks with a matching tempo and vibe. The confidence scores make the choice robust and let the player read the overall mood of a whole group and move between tracks as it changes, with no manual input.

Where performance drops

A. The face is not detected

Faces at the edge of the image (even in good light); low light with partial occlusion (even when the face is centred); many faces at different distances, where only some are found.

B. The face is found, the emotion is wrong

Weakly expressed emotions get confused, and another emotion may come out as dominant. The model recognises the frequent emotions (happiness, sadness, anger) far better and tends to give them higher scores.

Proposed improvements

  • Data augmentation (random noise, tilts, lighting changes) and more diverse training sets, against the bias towards centred, well-lit faces.
  • Temporal smoothing in real time: average the emotion scores over several frames at a fixed spacing, or take a majority vote of the dominant emotion over a sequence, to remove momentary errors.

3. Emotions across a video

To visualise the distribution, the system analysed sampled frames of a 30-second video. The report plots the emotion scores over time and the total score of each emotion. Neutral accumulates the largest total, followed by angry, sad, happy and fear; surprise and disgust stay near zero.

4. Language-model descriptions

The descriptions are written by Mistral-7B-Instruct: version v0.2 through the Hugging Face Hub, and v0.3 locally so that perplexity could be computed (the Hub does not expose token probabilities). Locally the model is loaded with 4-bit quantisation to fit in the available VRAM without a noticeable loss in output quality. Its input is a structured text describing the detected faces and their attributes.

Prompt design

The same detection (one man of about 35, fear, centre) was described with four prompt styles. Each response was scored with BERTScore F1 against a reference sentence and with the model's perplexity.

One- and few-shot prompts give higher BERTScore, i.e. better semantic correspondence with the reference text; few-shot produces the clearest text but also the least creative and varied. BERTScore and perplexity do not always agree, which is expected: BERTScore measures semantic closeness to a reference, perplexity the model's linguistic confidence. In every case Mistral produced comprehensible descriptions consistent with the detections.

Zero-shot: prompt and response
User: Describe the people detected in here: Face 1: age~35, gender=Man, dominant_emotion=fear, position=center
Response: In this description, we have one individual identified as follows: Face 1 (Center): - Age estimate: Approximately 35 years old. - Gender: Male. - Dominant emotion: Fear. This could suggest that the person may be feeling anxious or apprehensive in the current situation. It is important to note that facial expressions can change rapidly and this assessment should not be taken as a definitive measure of their emotional state at all times. - Position: The individual is located at the center of the scene.

5. Flask web application

The application separates logic from presentation. The core/ folder holds the full processing pipeline: face detection; emotion, age and gender analysis; and the language-model description. app.py connects that backend to the interface: it runs the pipeline on every uploaded image, passes the results to the templates and handles errors such as invalid files, slow processing and failed language-model responses.

The pages are Jinja2 templates that all inherit from base.html, with CSS for a clean, responsive layout of the annotated images and generated descriptions. The application is deployed on Hugging Face Spaces.
