The Emergence of Internal Representations in Sequence Models

Comparison of Architectures and Training Objectives

Summary

Can a sequence model form a world model of an environment it never observes directly? In Othello, models trained only on sequences of legal moves, with no supervision of the board, have been shown to develop representations from which the board state can be read out. This thesis asks whether that finding holds beyond a single Transformer trained on next-move prediction. It crosses two architectures (Transformer and Mamba) with two training objectives (autoregressive and JEPA-like) on 8×8, 12×12 and 16×16 boards: twelve conditions with shared data, parameter counts matched to within 0.86% and one evaluation protocol. Every model is examined at three levels: behaviour, decodability of the board state, and causal use of the represented state.

Main findings

  • No universal winner. Mamba-AR has the highest top-1 legality at every board size; Mamba-JEPA has the highest linear decodability of the relative board state at every board size.
  • The objective's effect depends on the architecture. Moving from AR to JEPA raises Mamba's macro-balanced decoding (+0.19, +1.48 and +0.42 pp at 8×8, 12×12 and 16×16) and lowers the Transformer's (−0.19, −0.69 and −4.51 pp). Over five independent seeds the interaction grows from +0.59 pp at 8×8 to +5.09 pp at 16×16.
  • JEPA stays close to AR in behaviour. Without an additional cross-entropy term over the move vocabulary during encoder training, the JEPA systems reach top-1 legality within 1.45 pp of AR through a head fitted afterwards on the frozen encoder.
  • Scale exposes what 8×8 hides. Top-1 legality is nearly saturated, but legal probability mass and board decoding fall sharply on the larger boards, and the differences between systems widen.
  • Part of the represented state is used. The adapted intervention raised the probability mass on the counterfactual board's legal moves in all twelve conditions (5.97–6.37 pp at 8×8, 0.78–1.48 pp at 16×16) and outperformed a geometrically matched random control; the effect weakens as the board grows.

1. Task, design and protocol

Each game is given to the model only as a sequence of square tokens. The board, the rules and the meaning of the tokens are never shown. Because the true state can be reconstructed exactly from the moves, every claim about what a model has represented can be checked against a known reference. Forced passes are not tokens; they follow from the absence of a legal move.

Data

For every board size there are 238 shards of about 100,000 random but fully legal games, in which each move is drawn uniformly from the legal set. The 8×8 corpus is the synthetic set released with Li et al.; the 12×12 and 16×16 corpora were generated deterministically with the same procedure. The vocabulary has n² − 4 move tokens plus padding, and the longest games have 60, 140 and 252 moves. The shard split is fixed before any training or selection:

Training

All twelve main runs use AdamW (learning rate 3·10⁻⁴, β = 0.9/0.95, weight decay 0.01), 1,000 warm-up steps followed by cosine decay to 10%, batch size 256 (78,200 steps), gradient clipping at 1.0 and bf16, with seed 42. Seeds 0–3 add four further independent runs at 8×8 and 16×16. Training took about 54 GPU-hours in total on one NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 (0.68 h for Transformer-AR at 8×8 up to 11.40 h for Mamba-JEPA at 16×16); evaluation ran separately on an NVIDIA L4.

Autoregressive (AR)

The encoder and its native output projection are trained jointly with cross-entropy on the next move, at every valid position of the shifted sequence.

JEPA-like

A context encoder reads the prefix; a linear predictor (512→512) predicts the representation of the next move, which is produced by an EMA target encoder (momentum 0.996) that receives only that move (hard-disjoint view). The loss is InfoNCE (τ = 0.1) with negatives from other games of the batch at the same absolute position, applied at every valid position. There is no cross-entropy over the move vocabulary while the encoder is trained.

Three levels of evaluation

  • Behaviour. Is the top prediction legal in the true current state? Also P@k, total probability on legal moves (legal mass) and performance by game phase, on 50,000 test games. AR models use their native head; JEPA encoders get a frozen linear or MLP head trained afterwards and selected on validation.
  • Decodability. Linear and MLP probes on every layer, for the absolute (black / white / empty) and relative (mine / yours / empty, w.r.t. the player to move) encodings; layer selected on validation by macro-balanced accuracy.
  • Causal use. Does editing the represented board shift the prediction towards the moves that are legal on the edited board?

2. Building the JEPA objective

The JEPA recipe comes from vision and did not transfer directly to discrete move sequences. A sequence of versions (v1–v6) tested what the target encoder should see, how far ahead to predict, which predictor and which loss to use, and how many positions of each game should provide a training signal. Two changes made the difference, and only together: removing the shared prefix from the target encoder's input (hard-disjoint views) and supervising every valid position instead of one random boundary per game.

3. Behaviour: legal next moves

JEPA systems use the frozen head chosen on validation by legal probability mass (linear everywhere except Mamba-JEPA at 8×8, where the MLP head won). 6. Causal interventions

Each case takes a prefix (from move 4 to two moves before the end) and flips the colour of one occupied square; it is kept only if the edited board still has legal moves and its legal set differs from the original. Per board size, 200 cases are used only for selection and 1,000 different cases for the final evaluation. Three protocols are compared:

  • Li: optimises the internal representation at selected depths so that a nonlinear probe of the absolute state reads the edited board.
  • Nanda: adds the normalised target-class direction of a raw linear probe of the relative state, with a fixed strength, at selected residual branches.
  • Adapted (this thesis): adds the difference between the target-class and source-class directions after every block, scaled by the standard deviation of the representation; the strength α ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8} is chosen on the 200 selection cases. It is also compared with a geometrically matched random control (one shared random permutation and sign flip of the coordinates, preserving norms and angles).

The published sites and constants are kept for Transformer-AR at 8×8; elsewhere the sites are mapped by relative depth, so JEPA, Mamba and the larger boards are explicit extensions rather than replications.

  • The adapted intervention was the only method with a substantive positive effect across architectures and objectives, and the largest gain in legal mass at every size: 5.97–6.37 pp at 8×8, 2.19–2.72 pp at 12×12 and 0.78–1.48 pp at 16×16.
  • Nanda gave weak changes (at most 0.7 pp), practically zero in the Mamba conditions. Li was effective essentially only on the two 8×8 Transformers (about +2.3 pp), almost inert on Mamba and sometimes negative on the larger boards; this is consistent with intervention sites and settings not transferring automatically across architectures and scales.
  • The adapted method also reduced the false positives plus false negatives among the top-N predictions by 0.45–2.17 per case and improved top-1 legality on the edited board in eleven of twelve conditions (the exception: −0.10 pp on Transformer-JEPA 16×16).

Read the ranking of methods with care: only the adapted method had its strength tuned on validation, and it intervenes at more sites. No paired uncertainty intervals were computed for the interventions, and some differences are very small: the +0.40 pp top-1 advantage over the random control on Transformer-JEPA 16×16 is a net difference of four successes in 1,000 cases. Larger boards also have more legal moves, so flipping one square changes a smaller part of the legal set and leaves less room for improvement.

7. What changes with board size

Moving to larger boards is the largest source of variation in the study. Top-1 legality hardly moves in absolute terms, but legal probability mass, board decoding and the intervention effects all fall, and the differences between systems grow.

  • The gap between the best and worst system grows from 0.43 to 1.51 pp in top-1 legality and from 1.32 to 5.39 pp in linear board decoding between 8×8 and 16×16.
  • Expressed as distance from 100%, the top-1 error multiplies by 3.7–6.2, the probability on illegal moves by 5.4–14.1 and the decoding error by 5.5–8.3 from 8×8 to 16×16. These ratios are inflated by the very small starting errors, but they show that top-1 looks robust largely because it starts close to its ceiling.
  • Transformer-JEPA is the most scale-sensitive system: it loses 18.4 pp of decoding accuracy, against 14.0–14.5 pp for the other three, and it also shows the largest drop in top-1 legality.
  • Scale is not a single change: positions, vocabulary, game length and the number of reachable states all grow while dmodel stays at 512. The comparisons within a board size remain like-for-like.

8. Limitations

  • The games are random legal games, so legality measures learning of the rules, not strategic quality. Othello is synthetic, fully observed and deterministic; the same representations need not appear in partially observable or stochastic settings.
  • Parameter matching required different depths (8 vs 15 blocks), so the processing mechanism is not separated from the number of layers. One final JEPA configuration was studied, not the whole family of objectives.
  • The AR native head and the JEPA frozen head are different learning paths, so behavioural differences of the complete systems do not isolate the effect of the objective on the encoder.
  • Probes show that information can be recovered, not that the model uses it. On large boards part of the decoding drop may reflect the capacity of the linear probe, and because intervention directions come from probes, smaller causal effects may partly reflect less accurate directions.
  • Interventions test the immediately following prediction after a local edit, not whether the model tracks the edited state over later moves.
  • Five seeds were run at 8×8 and 16×16 but not at 12×12; bootstrap intervals describe only the variability of the test sample.
